## Predict

A **pinion** is a small gear. A **rack** is a straight bar with teeth along one edge. When they mesh, turning the pinion slides the rack along.

Talk with a partner:

- When you turn the pinion clockwise, which way will the rack slide?
- The pinion has 12 teeth. How many teeth along the rack do you think one turn will move it?

## Run

1. Print the **rack and pinion** template (check the square measures 50 mm) and glue it onto thick card.
2. Cut out the base, the pinion (tooth by tooth), the rack and the fence.
3. Glue the fence along the **bottom edge** of the base. Ask an adult to make the axle hole in the base.
4. Cut a 4 cm piece of skewer, push it up through the hole and fix it underneath with Prestik.
5. Lay the rack on the base with its flat edge against the fence and its teeth upwards. Put a short piece of straw on the skewer, then the pinion, so its teeth mesh with the rack.
6. Draw an arrow on the pinion from the centre to the edge. Turn it slowly and watch the rack.

## Investigate

1. Turn the pinion clockwise. Which way does the rack slide? Turn it the other way.
2. Slide the rack so its end is at the pinion. Draw a pencil line on the base at the other end of the rack. Turn the pinion exactly **one** turn and measure how far the rack moved, in millimetres.
3. Count how many rack teeth went past the pinion in one turn.
4. Push the rack with your finger instead. What does the pinion do?

## Make

Use your rack and pinion in a machine that needs straight movement: a sliding door for a model house, a drawbridge that slides out, or a pointer that moves along a scale to show a number. Draw your design, show which way to turn the pinion, then build and test it.

## Teacher notes

- **Timing**: 10 min predict, 25 min build, 15 min investigate and share.
- **Big idea**: a rack and pinion changes rotary (round) motion into linear (straight) motion. One turn moves the rack by the pinion's number of teeth times the tooth spacing (here 12 × 9 mm = 108 mm). It works both ways. The rack has 16 teeth (144 mm), so it travels a little more than one turn of the pinion: start each test with the rack's end at the pinion.
- **Common problems**: the rack slips past the pinion (the axle hole is too high: check it against the template, or press the rack against the fence); the rack lifts up (tape a loose strip of card over its far end as a guide).
- **Real world**: car steering, some sliding gates, and the focus knob on a microscope.
- **Machines link**: the Rack and pinion topic has the picture and a quick test.
